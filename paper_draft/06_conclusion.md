# 6. Conclusion

This study has established a quantitative, data-driven framework to evaluate the biological, technical, and historical drivers behind *Escherichia coli*'s premier status as a model microorganism. By applying large language model-augmented claim extraction and unsupervised semantic clustering to 3,273 publications, the conceptual architecture that defines how the scientific community justifies model organism selection has been mapped.

The findings from this study reveal that *E. coli*'s dominance is sustained by a unique combination of institutionalization and multi-dimensional utility. The discovery of the **Axiomatic Acceptance Gap**—where 61.5% of *E. coli* papers contain no explicit justification compared to ~31% for eukaryotic and Gram-positive controls—proves that the bacterium operates as a self-evident axiom in molecular biology. Unlike Yeast and Bacillus, which are specialized niche models restricted to representing eukaryotic cellular pathways (99.0% of yeast claims) or Gram-positive spore-forming physiology (98.1% of Bacillus claims), *E. coli* exhibits a balanced, general-purpose profile distributed across nine distinct research themes.

Temporally, this analysis captures a major paradigm shift. Over the past 35 years, foundational justifications related to basic DNA replication and recombination have declined, and heterologous protein expression has transitioned from an active area of justification (44.1% in the 1990s) into an implicit utility (12.6% in the 2020s). This technical normalization has been balanced by the continuous rise of *E. coli* as the definitive bacterial reference standard (rising from 2.9% to 30.3%), catalyzed by the completion of its genome sequence in 1997. Mapped biological annotations confirm that these justifications are anchored in physical, highly conserved cellular machineries (e.g., DNA polymerase III, Sec/Tat translocons, and the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump).

Beyond microbiology, this work demonstrates the power of computational epistemology. Combining LLM-based zero-shot extraction with semantic vector spaces provides a scalable methodology for identifying the "unspoken consensus" and historical trajectories that shape scientific fields. As biology moves toward non-model systems and synthetic hosts, the computational framework developed here offers a template to track, evaluate, and direct the evolution of future model systems.
